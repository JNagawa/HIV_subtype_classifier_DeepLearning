🧬 Why HIV-1 Subtype Classification Matters

The Challenge

  • HIV-1 has extreme genetic diversity across 9+ subtypes
  • Subtypes A and D co-circulate in East Africa
  • Subtype influences drug efficacy and disease progression
  • Traditional subtyping requires slow, expensive sequence alignment

Our Goal

  • Classify pol gene sequences into subtypes A, B, C, D
  • Use raw nucleotide data — no alignment or k-mers
  • Enable rapid, unaligned-sequence molecular surveillance

Clinical Relevance

  • Epidemiological surveillance — tracking subtype spread
  • Treatment optimization — drug efficacy varies by subtype
  • Decentralized testing — no server needed
  • Resource-limited settings — instant classification

📊 Dataset: LANL HIV Sequence Database

Source

  • Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) — gold-standard HIV database
  • Accessions from Solis-Reyes et al. (2018) Kameris paper
  • Not Kaggle or Zindi

Data Pipeline

  • 9,270 accessions requested → 9,264 retrieved
  • Filtered to pure subtypes A, B, C, D → 5,588
  • Removed duplicates → 5,564 final sequences

⚠️ Subtype D: Complete failure
All 11 test D sequences classified as C. Only 53 training samples — insufficient for deep learning.

Why Macro F1 ≠ Accuracy

  • Accuracy (98.44%) reflects weighted performance
  • Macro F1 (0.7421) penalizes equally per-class
  • D's 0.00 F1 drags macro average down
  • Weighted F1 = 0.9780 (more realistic)

Implications

  • Model is excellent for A, B, C classification
  • Model is unusable for subtype D
  • Root cause: data scarcity, not architecture

Error Confidence

  • Mean confidence on errors: 0.709
  • Median confidence on errors: 0.786
  • These are systematic, not borderline

Biological Explanation

  • Subtypes C and D share significant phylogenetic similarity in the pol gene
  • Both co-circulate in East Africa — possible recombination
  • With only 53 training samples for D vs. 1,612 for C (30:1 ratio), the model cannot learn D-specific motifs

Why CNN > BiLSTM

  • Subtype discrimination is driven by local nucleotide motifs
  • CNN's convolutional filters detect these motifs naturally
  • BiLSTM's long-range modeling adds parameters without benefit
  • Consistent with known biology — subtype mutations cluster locally

⚖️ Ethical Considerations

Data Privacy

  • All sequences from public LANL database
  • No patient-identifiable information
  • Identified by GenBank accession numbers only

Sampling Bias

  • Subtype B overrepresented (Western sequencing bias)
  • Subtypes A and D underrepresented despite East African importance
  • Model performance directly reflects this inequity

Fairness Implications

  • Model achieves near-perfect accuracy for well-sampled subtypes (B, C)
  • But completely fails for subtype D — the subtype most relevant to East African clinical settings
  • This mirrors a broader pattern: communities most affected by HIV are least represented in genomic databases

Responsible Use

  • Explicit disclaimers in deployment interface
  • Not for clinical decision-making
  • Research and educational purposes only

⚠️ Limitations & Future Work

Current Limitations

  • Severe class imbalance (D: only 75 sequences)
  • Only 4 of 9+ subtypes classified
  • No CRF or recombinant detection
  • Gap-stripped ≠ truly unaligned data
  • DNABERT-2 failed (O(L²) memory)
  • No explainability (Grad-CAM, attention)
  • No transfer learning from pre-trained models
  • Single gene region (pol only)

Future Directions

  • Data augmentation for rare subtypes (synthetic sequences)
  • Multi-gene classification (gag + pol + env)
  • Extend to all subtypes and CRFs
  • Explainability: Grad-CAM filter visualization
  • Efficient transformers (FlashAttention)
  • True unaligned sequence evaluation
  • Clinical validation in East African settings

💡 Lessons Learned

Technical Insights

1D-CNN is ideal for genomic motif detection — it naturally learns local nucleotide patterns that define subtypes.
BiLSTM adds parameters (6.5×) without commensurate benefit — subtype signal is primarily local, not long-range.
Class-weighted loss helps but cannot solve extreme imbalance (30:1 ratio). Data scarcity is the bottleneck.

Broader Reflections

Deep learning can replace alignment-based subtyping for well-sampled classes, enabling faster molecular surveillance.
Model performance mirrors dataset bias — equitable genomic sampling is a prerequisite for equitable AI.
Deployment with appropriate disclaimers bridges the gap between research and practical tools.
